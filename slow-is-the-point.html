Why it's built this way

You're strong, 210 lb, and have never trained cardio. Your heart and lungs will adapt within about three weeks. Your Achilles tendons, shins and plantar fascia take six to twelve months. Everything in this plan sits inside that gap, because that gap is what ends most beginner running.

Volume injures beginners, not effort

In a randomized trial of novice runners in your BMI range, starting at 3 km a week gave a 6.9% injury rate over four weeks. Starting at 6 km gave 18.5%. Nearly triple, for one extra kilometre per session.

Walk breaks are the training

Not a scaffold to abandon early. They're what lets you accumulate running minutes at a load your body can repair before the next session. Take them on the days you feel great — especially then.

Full sentences, always

You should be able to say your address out loud while running. If you can't, slow down. Effort sits at 4 or 5 out of 10 every single time. This is the biggest lever and the easiest to get wrong.

Minutes, never distance

Chasing a distance makes you push pace to finish sooner. Standard Couch-to-5K jumps from 5 to 20 minutes of running in week 5 — that jump is where most people quit or get hurt. There's no jump like it here.

What to add to your lifting

Keep training as you are — squats and deadlifts are doing you favors. Add these twice a week. They cover the tissue running loads and lifting doesn't.

Calf raises, straight-leg

3 × 15 → single leg → weighted

The Achilles takes the most load per stride and is the most common early casualty. Slow, full range, three-second lower.

Calf raises, bent-knee

3 × 15, knee bent ~30°

Hits the soleus, which carries most of the load at running pace and is routinely undertrained by lifters.

Tibialis raises

3 × 20, back to a wall

Toes up against resistance. The best insurance against shin splints, the injury most likely to interrupt weeks 4–10.

Single-leg RDL

3 × 8 per side, light

Running is a series of single-leg landings. Teaches your hip to control one, which bilateral lifts never asked for.

Step-downs

3 × 10 per side, 6–12" box

Slow eccentric control at the knee. Protective against the runner's-knee pattern that appears as volume climbs.

Side plank & dead bug

3 × 30–45s each

Trunk stability under fatigue. Form breaking down at minute 25 is what turns a long run into an injury.

Don't lift legs heavy the day before a run. Sore calves change how you land, and changed landing is how beginners get hurt. If the schedule forces overlap, lift legs on the same day as a run rather than the day before.

Reading your body

Keep going

Soreness spread across a whole muscle group. Aching that shows up during a run and fades within it. Morning stiffness that loosens in ten minutes. This is adaptation and it's supposed to happen.

Repeat the week, don't advance

Discomfort still there the next morning. Anything that changes how you land, even slightly. Finishing wrecked rather than like you had more in you. Repeating a week costs seven days; an injury costs eight weeks.

Stop and get it looked at

Sharp or stabbing pain. Pain you can cover with one fingertip, especially over bone along the shin or top of the foot. Anything that makes you limp. Pain that worsens as the run goes on. Swelling. A bone stress injury caught early is weeks, not months.

Practical

  • Shoes, properly fitted. A neutral cushioned trainer from an actual running store — they usually run a half to full size larger than street shoes. At your weight this is the one piece of gear worth money. Replace at 300–500 miles.
  • Treadmill first, outdoors later. A treadmill takes pace out of your hands, which is your main risk. Walk at 3.0–3.5 mph, jog at 4.2–4.8, incline 1%. From week 6, move one run a week outside; by week 16, most of them.
  • Cadence. Shorter, quicker steps — around 170–180 a minute — land more gently. Don't obsess; just notice if you're reaching out with a long stride.
  • Form: don't overthink it. Look 20 feet ahead, loose shoulders, short quick steps, arms swinging. That's the list. Do not try to change your footstrike.
  • Weight. Every pound off is less force at each of thousands of footstrikes. Worth doing gradually — but don't cut calories hard while adding new training load.
  • If you miss a week, repeat the last week you finished. Miss two or more, drop back two weeks.
  • One new variable at a time. Not a fourth run day, new shoes and hills in the same week — when something hurts you won't know which caused it.

On the doctor question. Starting from where you are is normal and safe. But with a family history of early heart disease, or any chest pressure, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or palpitations during a session — stop and get it checked rather than pushing through.

The honest road to a marathon

Months 1–4

This plan

Run/walk to a continuous 30 minutes. The goal isn't fitness — it's teaching your legs to absorb impact and building the habit of three sessions a week.

Months 5–8

Base building

Four runs a week. One long run creeping from 40 minutes toward 75. Roughly 20–25 miles a week, almost all easy. Race a 10K for fun.

Months 9–13

Half marathon

A 12–14 week block off that base. The first distance where a marathon feels real rather than theoretical — and a great race to run together.

Months 14–20

Marathon block

16–20 weeks, peaking around 35–45 miles a week with a 20-mile long run. Pick the race six months out so the block has a date attached.

Realistically 18 to 24 months from today. Novice injury rates run far above experienced ones, and the strongest predictor of finishing a marathon is an uninterrupted year of running beforehand. You could compress it to about 14 months if everything went perfectly; most people who try lose more time to injury than they saved.

The nearer prize: within about six months you can join her easy runs. Her easy pace and your hard pace overlap long before your race paces do, and easy running is most of what marathon training actually is.
